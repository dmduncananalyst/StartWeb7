# When TikTok Became a Search Engine

TikTok has become a place where people search for recommendations, experiences, and practical demonstrations. In Adobe Express’s 2026 survey, 49 percent of consumer respondents said they had used it as a search engine, compared with 41 percent in the 2024 survey. That growth reflects a changing search habit, rather than proof that TikTok has replaced Google.

## The confession that started the story

Companies rarely praise a rival in public. That is why a moment from July 2022 still stands out. Speaking at Fortune's Brainstorm Tech conference, Prabhakar Raghavan, the Google senior vice president who ran Knowledge and Information, talked openly about a habit his company was seeing in its own studies.

New internet users, he said, did not have the expectations older users had grown up with. They did not tend to type keywords. They looked to discover content in more immersive ways. Then came the number that made headlines. In Google's studies, almost 40 percent of young people, when looking for a place for lunch, did not go to Google Maps or Search. They went to TikTok or Instagram.

Google confirmed the figure came from internal research involving United States users ages 18 to 24. The admission mattered more than the spreadsheet. The company that had turned its own name into a verb for searching was saying, on stage, that a generation was starting somewhere else.

Raghavan also noted that young people had never seen a paper map, yet maps products were designed to look like one stuck on a phone. The problem was not a missing feature. It was a different mental model of what finding something should feel like.

## Entertainment becomes discovery

TikTok is strongly associated with entertainment, but watching and searching can overlap. People were already watching strangers cook, travel, fix things, review products, and walk through neighborhoods. A video can spark a question, and the search box offers a way to keep exploring it.

The content had a quality text results often lacked. You could see the restaurant, not just read its name and rating. You could watch a dish arrive, hear the room, and judge the portion with your own eyes. A written review asks for trust. A video from the actual place feels, fairly or not, closer to evidence.

That helps explain why visual topics fit the experience. Recipes, product demonstrations, and visits to restaurants all benefit from seeing something happen. A video can show texture, movement, or atmosphere in ways a short written description cannot.

## What the numbers say now

Four years after Raghavan’s comments, Adobe Express surveyed 807 consumers and 200 small business owners in the United States. Its consumer findings suggest that TikTok search is more than a passing curiosity. The figures describe that survey sample, rather than a count of everyone in the country.

The sample also matters when interpreting the research. More than half of the consumer respondents were millennials, and different generations were represented in different proportions. Comparing two surveys is useful, but it does not show that every age group changed its behavior in exactly the same way.

Respondents cited short videos, storytelling, and interaction as reasons to choose TikTok. Tutorials and product reviews were popular formats. That combination helps explain the appeal of seeing a person demonstrate something, rather than reading a description alone.

Read together, those numbers suggest people are not only looking for facts on TikTok. They are looking for a person. A fact can be copied anywhere. A person showing what happened when they tried something carries a texture a list of links struggles to match.

## The plot twist in the data

Here is where the story gets more interesting than the headlines. Adobe’s survey found that Gen Z respondents remained especially likely to prefer Google overall. Using TikTok sometimes and choosing it as a primary search tool are different behaviors.

In other words, search did not move from one winner to another. It splintered. A young person might start a restaurant hunt on TikTok, check a debate on Reddit, ask a chatbot to summarize options, and still open Google for hours and directions. The old idea of a single front door to the internet is fading.

That mix changes what people expect from search. A person may want a demonstration for one task, a detailed document for another, and a map for the journey itself. No single format is equally useful for every situation.

Trust complicates the picture. A convincing video can make an experience feel tangible without proving that its claims are accurate. Popularity, presentation, and expertise are different things, even when they appear together on the same screen.

## A search box with a camera roll

Step back and the larger shift comes into focus. For decades, search meant words in a box, matched against words on pages. Social search breaks that symmetry. The query is still words, but the answer is a face, a voice, a room, a meal. The unit of information changed from the document to the moment.

That change also complicates how people judge authority. Someone who films an experience convincingly may attract attention without having expertise in the subject. A personal demonstration can be useful evidence of one experience, but it is not automatically reliable evidence of a general claim.

Traditional search and social search can also meet in the same journey. Someone may watch a video, look up the business behind it, and check its website before making contact. The choice is often about which tool suits the immediate task.

Whether TikTok remains central to social search is a separate question. Platforms can change, but the attraction of searching by watching is easy to understand. People want information they can inspect, relate to, and place in the context of everyday life.

The search box is still there. It now shares attention with feeds that can turn a casual moment of watching into a deliberate search.

# The Cookie That Would Not Die, Inside Google’s Five Year Reversal

In January 2020, Google announced that it intended to phase out third party cookies in Chrome within two years. After repeated delays, it changed course in July 2024 and confirmed in April 2025 that it would keep its existing approach to cookie choice without a new standalone prompt. The cookie survived, and later in 2025 Google announced the retirement of several proposed replacement technologies.

## A small file with an enormous job

A third party cookie is a tiny file placed on your browser by a domain other than the site you are visiting. It sounds trivial. It became the plumbing of the modern ad economy. Because the same advertising company could place and read its cookie across thousands of unrelated sites, it could follow a person around the web, build a picture of their interests, and decide which ads to show them and what those ads were worth.

That plumbing was always controversial. Tracking across unrelated sites raises privacy concerns because a person may not understand which companies are collecting information or how it is being used. Different browsers responded with different protections.

In March 2020, Apple’s WebKit team announced full third party cookie blocking in Safari. Firefox also adopted protections against third party tracking cookies. Those distinctions matter, blocking tracking cookies and blocking every third party cookie are not necessarily the same policy.

## The promise, made with a date attached

Google had launched its Privacy Sandbox initiative in August 2019, presenting it as a way to protect privacy while keeping an ad supported web alive. Then, on January 14, 2020, Chrome engineering director Justin Schuh put a date on the future. Google intended to phase out support for third party cookies in Chrome within two years.

Google argued that simply blocking cookies could encourage less visible workarounds, including fingerprinting. It wanted alternatives that could support advertising while improving privacy. Its announcement presented the two year timeline as an intention dependent on developing those alternatives, rather than an unconditional deadline.

The replacements had memorable names and difficult births. One early proposal, Federated Learning of Cohorts, or FLoC, would group people with similar browsing into cohorts advertisers could target. Critics argued it could still expose sensitive interests and handed even more power to the browser maker. FLoC was later dropped in favor of the Topics API, which had the browser note general topics of interest instead. Each new idea arrived with fresh arguments about whether it worked well enough, and for whom.

## The regulator in the room

Google also faced competition concerns. Britain’s Competition and Markets Authority opened an investigation in January 2021 into whether the proposed changes could distort competition. That scrutiny made the story about more than browser engineering.

In February 2022, the regulator accepted commitments governing Google’s development of the proposals. Privacy protections, the effect on competing companies, and the practical operation of advertising all had to be considered together.

Meanwhile, the deadline began its long slide. In June 2021, Google pushed the phase out to late 2023. In July 2022, the target moved to the second half of 2024. In April 2024, it slipped to 2025 at the earliest, pending regulators. An industry that had reorganized around the original two year promise was now four years in, still preparing, and increasingly unsure the day would come.

## The reversal arrives in two acts

Act one came on July 22, 2024. Anthony Chavez, Google's vice president for the Privacy Sandbox, announced that Google would no longer deprecate third party cookies. Instead, Google proposed an updated approach built around user choice. Chrome would introduce a new experience letting people make an informed choice that applied across their browsing, and adjust that choice at any time.

The language was careful, but the direction had changed. Instead of ending support for third party cookies, Google proposed an experience through which users could make a choice across their browsing. It acknowledged the work involved in a transition and the effect on publishers and advertisers.

Act two was quieter. On April 22, 2025, Chavez announced Google would maintain its current approach to third party cookie choice in Chrome, and would not roll out a new standalone prompt. Users could keep making choices in Chrome's Privacy and Security Settings, as before. The grand prompt, centerpiece of the user choice plan, was gone less than a year after it was proposed.

The story continued after the prompt was dropped. On October 17, 2025, Google announced that it would retire several Privacy Sandbox technologies, including Topics, Protected Audience, and Attribution Reporting. It said low adoption and feedback about their expected value informed the decision, while other technologies would continue to receive support.

## What five years of preparing left behind

It would be too simple to say nothing changed. Browser privacy protections continued to differ, and the discussion brought more attention to the relationship between tracking and the advertising that supports many websites. The reversal did not erase the underlying privacy concerns.

Repeated changes can make preparation difficult. A publisher or advertiser planning around a browser update has to decide what to build before every detail is settled. This story shows how a roadmap from one widely used browser can influence decisions across the web, even when that roadmap later changes.

The privacy debate itself never resolved. Critics of the reversal argue that keeping cookies in the dominant browser preserves a system of cross site tracking that its rivals abandoned years ago, and that burying the choice in settings most people never open is choice in name only. Defenders of Google's caution argue that a badly designed replacement could have entrenched Google further, damaged the open web's economics, and pushed tracking into less visible forms. Both arguments can be true at once, which is part of why the argument never ended.

What is certain is the strangeness of the ending. The third party cookie was formally sentenced in 2020, granted delay after delay, and then quietly reprieved. It survives not because anyone declared it good, but because replacing it turned out to be harder and riskier than ending it ever sounded in a blog post.

The cookie survived the planned phase out. The larger argument about privacy, advertising, and the economics of the web remains unresolved.

# Wikipedia Is Feeding the AI Era While Losing Its Readers

Wikipedia reported a decline in human pageviews even as its content remained important to AI systems and other online services. In October 2025, the Wikimedia Foundation described a roughly 8 percent decline over recent months compared with the same months in 2024, after revising its bot detection. It identified generative AI and social media as likely influences, rather than proving that AI alone caused the change.

## The discovery hidden in a traffic spike

The traffic figure emerged after Wikimedia investigated an apparent surge in visits and revised how it distinguished people from automated systems. That distinction matters for understanding an audience. A busy server does not necessarily mean more people are reading the encyclopedia.

The revised figures covered a defined period, not a complete year of Wikipedia traffic. The Foundation cautioned that changes in detection rules affect how comparisons should be interpreted. The important finding was a decline in estimated human reading beneath some traffic that had initially appeared to be genuine.

The Foundation published its interpretation in an October 17, 2025 post by Marshall Miller. It described a challenge for a knowledge project whose work can reach people through other products without those people visiting the original articles.

## Used everywhere, visited less

That paradox deserves a slow look. Knowledge written by Wikipedia volunteers can reach someone through a search summary or an AI tool. Reading information based on an encyclopedia and visiting the encyclopedia itself are different events.

The Foundation pointed to changing search and social media habits as possible explanations. A summary may satisfy a reader before they open a source, while a social platform can keep discovery inside its own interface. These are plausible mechanisms, but the traffic comparison does not isolate their individual effects.

There is a particular sting in this for Wikipedia. It is more than a collection of pages, it is a project that depends on participation. Someone who arrives to read may later correct an error, contribute an article, or support the nonprofit behind the site. A smaller direct audience could affect those opportunities, although a traffic decline alone does not establish a matching decline in editors or donations.

That is the tension at the heart of the story. Wider distribution can bring knowledge to more people while making it harder for the original project to build a relationship with those readers. Both things can happen at the same time.

## The volunteer bargain meets the chatbot

To understand why this moment feels different from earlier web shifts, it helps to remember the bargain Wikipedia has run on since 2001. The encyclopedia reached its twenty fifth anniversary in January 2026. Across that history, volunteers have written and maintained articles that other people can read, question, correct, and expand. Readers can become contributors, and that participation helps keep knowledge current.

Search engines have long helped people find Wikipedia articles. Opening a result brings the reader into a space where the article’s references, revision history, and surrounding discussion can be inspected. That is a different experience from receiving a short summary somewhere else.

Generative AI changes that relationship when a summary becomes the destination. Some systems include citations and links, but a reader may never open them. Convenience is valuable, yet a useful summary and a continuing connection to the source are separate outcomes.

It is not a question Wikipedia asks only about itself. Publishers, reference sites, forums, and independent blogs can face similar tensions between distribution and direct visits. Wikipedia makes the issue particularly visible because the encyclopedia is freely accessible and much of its work comes from volunteers.

## Pushback, experiments, and an unresolved argument

Wikimedia has responded through work on responsible content access, attribution, and the reading experience. Wikimedia Enterprise also offers services for organizations reusing content at scale. That paid service is distinct from charging readers to access the encyclopedia, and it is one reason the project’s funding model should not be described as having no commercial service at all.

The wider argument remains unsettled. Reaching people through other products can support the mission of sharing knowledge. Sustaining the people and infrastructure behind that knowledge is a different challenge. Neither the traffic figure nor the convenience of an AI summary settles how those two needs will be balanced.

What gives the story its weight is what Wikipedia represents. Strangers have built and maintained a free reference work at a global scale for decades. Its future depends on more than how often its information appears elsewhere, it also depends on whether people continue to inspect, improve, and support the original project.

The encyclopedia is still there, still updated every minute. The open question is whether the systems built on top of it will remember to send anyone back.
