What are red flags when choosing an SEO company?

Warning signs include promised Google positions, unclear ownership, reports that avoid business outcomes and an agreement that leaves responsibilities vague. A concern deserves an explanation supported by evidence. Results taking time is different from a provider refusing to answer reasonable questions.

A promised position is different from a plan

Google controls its unpaid results. A provider claiming an exclusive relationship with Google or a reserved place at the top is making a very different claim from offering professional website work. Google’s hiring guidance specifically warns about promised top rankings.

A clear service agreement explains the work, reporting and communication. Those details give you a useful way to compare proposals and review the service during the relationship.

Reports can look impressive without explaining sales

Charts, percentages and traffic totals can contain accurate information yet leave the business question unanswered. An owner may receive more visitors while the calendar remains empty. The report needs to explain what the numbers describe and what they do not establish.

Ask how the company connects its work with the calls, bookings, and paid sales you care about. A report should help you understand the business it is bringing in, not bury that question under an impressive activity total.

Confusing ownership creates avoidable dependence

A business should know who controls the domain, website and relevant accounts. A provider withholding access to the owner’s own assets raises a different issue from disappointing marketing results. It can make leaving the relationship unnecessarily difficult.

Ownership should be discussed while the agreement is being considered, not discovered during a dispute. The owner does not need to understand every technical permission, but should receive a clear explanation of control, ongoing subscriptions and the handover arrangement.

Pressure can hide unanswered questions

A deadline for a genuine business reason is different from pressure that prevents the owner from reading the agreement or comparing responsibilities. The provider should have room to explain what is included and what the customer remains responsible for.

Another warning sign is evidence with no context. A ranking screenshot or a revenue claim is difficult to evaluate without knowing which company, period and circumstances it describes. Previous work can be useful evidence, but it should not be presented as an automatic forecast for every client.

A difficult month is not automatically misconduct

Changes in performance are a useful topic for the reporting conversation. A clear summary of completed work and recorded activity gives that discussion concrete information.

The more serious concern is an inability or refusal to explain the agreed work, account ownership or the limits of a claim. An accountable relationship gives the owner enough information to evaluate progress. A relationship built entirely on pressure, vague promises and inaccessible accounts makes that evaluation much harder.
