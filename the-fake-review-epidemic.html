The Fake Review Epidemic, Why Online Trust Is Harder Than Ever

October 12, 2026

Fake reviews can distort the way people choose a business and undermine companies that earn their reputation honestly. Google says it blocked or removed more than 292 million policy violating reviews on Maps in 2025. That figure shows the scale of enforcement, but it does not establish what percentage of all online reviews are fake.

The stars influence real decisions

Before someone calls a company, they may look at what other customers have said. Reviews can make an unfamiliar business feel more approachable, or raise questions that affect whether the person goes any further. For a local company, those opinions can become part of its first impression.

That is what makes invented feedback so damaging. A review is valuable because it appears to describe a real experience. When the experience never happened, the reader is being asked to make a decision using evidence that does not exist.

What the Google numbers actually tell us

In its account of Maps protections for 2025, Google reported blocking or removing more than 292 million reviews that violated its policies, alongside publishing more than one billion helpful reviews. It also reported removing more than 13 million fake Business Profiles.

Those are enforcement totals, rather than a measurement of how many visible reviews are fake. A policy violation is not automatically the same thing as an invented customer experience, and blocked reviews do not necessarily appear publicly. Dividing the removal total by the publication total would not produce a reliable fraud rate.

AI complicates the trust problem

Tools that produce fluent writing can also be misused to invent believable accounts of products or services. A polished paragraph no longer provides much reassurance that the person writing it actually visited the business.

At the same time, writing style alone is not proof of fraud. A short review can be genuine, a detailed review can be false, and a real customer may use writing assistance to express a real experience. The important issue is whether the review truthfully represents what happened.

Honest businesses can be caught in the middle

Fabricated praise can create an unfair impression of one company, while fabricated complaints can damage another. Both interfere with the customer’s ability to judge a business on its actual work.

Google has described protections against review extortion, including temporarily pausing contributions when a profile receives a sudden spike in spam. A temporary restriction or a missing review should therefore not be treated as proof that the business bought reviews. The circumstances matter.

There are consequences beyond reputation

The Federal Trade Commission’s consumer review rule prohibits specified deceptive practices involving fake reviews and testimonials. It addresses creating or selling fake reviews, buying them under the conditions described in the rule, and incentives tied to a particular positive or negative sentiment. It allows the agency to seek civil penalties against knowing violators.

That does not make every suspicious review a proven legal violation, or every case subject to the same penalty. It does make clear that manufactured feedback is more than an ordinary marketing shortcut.

Trust cannot be reduced to a star total

Reviews are useful, but they remain one part of the picture. A rating cannot explain everything about the services a business offers, the people doing the work or whether the company is the right fit for a particular customer.

Genuine feedback deserves to remain meaningful. A company that has earned customer trust should be represented by real experiences, rather than competing with invented ones. The long term value of a reputation comes from what the business actually delivers.
