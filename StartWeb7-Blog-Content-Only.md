# How Craigslist Kept Its 1990s Look and Outlived Prettier Rivals

**Card photo:** `craigslist-kept-its-1990s-look.png`

Craigslist has kept a simple website built around familiar local listings while many online services have changed their appearance and business models. Its longevity reflects useful categories, an established audience, and a restrained approach to the experience. Its simple interface has become part of what returning visitors recognize.

Open Craigslist today and the first feeling for many visitors is time travel. There are no hero images sliding across the top, no rounded buttons in fashionable colors, no video playing behind the text. There is a white page, a modest logo, and column after column of blue links. For anyone who first used the internet in a school library or on a family computer in the kitchen, the page looks familiar in a way that almost nothing else on the modern web does.

That familiarity is the visible result of more than thirty years of choices. While other services added social features and more elaborate interfaces, Craigslist retained its recognizable structure. Researcher Jessa Lingel has argued that its usefulness and loyal audience help explain why it has faced less pressure to reinvent itself.

## An email list about San Francisco events

Craigslist began in 1995, founded by Craig Newmark as an email list sharing information about events in San Francisco. It was less a company launch than a community habit. Friends told friends about apartments, gatherings, jobs, and things for sale, and the list became a place where a city talked to itself.

The blue links are the most obvious part of that continuity. Categories remain visible without a visitor having to discover a new navigation system. Someone returning to look for an apartment, a bicycle, or contract work can recognize the basic task immediately. Familiarity removes a small amount of uncertainty before the search has even begun.

Craigslist’s own biography says Jim Buckmaster has led the company since 2000. His long tenure is part of the continuity behind the site. Interviews with people who have studied the company describe a leadership culture that places unusual weight on simplicity and its public service identity, rather than treating a redesign as an automatic sign of progress.

## The shark that never had to evolve

In a 2020 NPR interview, scholar Jessa Lingel used a shark as a metaphor for Craigslist’s longevity. Her point concerned a form that continues to suit its environment. A useful service with a loyal audience can face less pressure to reinvent its appearance simply because fashions change.

Lingel’s work also considers users who value Craigslist’s openness and low barriers. A service organized around practical listings offers a different experience from one organized around follower counts and personal feeds.

At the same time, critics have never been quiet. The same plainness that fans describe as honest, detractors describe as clunky, hard to search, and vulnerable to scams and spam. The debate has followed the site for decades, whether plain design protects trust or simply asks users to do extra work that better tools could handle for them.

## A restrained business model behind the plain page

The financial story makes the design story harder to dismiss as nostalgia. In its 2020 reporting, NPR referred to an outside consulting estimate that Craigslist’s revenue exceeded one billion dollars in 2018. That is a historical estimate, not a current financial statement from the privately held company. It also describes revenue, not profit.

The model explains part of the contrast. Craigslist charges for selected posting categories while much everyday browsing and posting remains free. Its own posting information distinguishes those categories. The business has not been built around filling the page with the banner advertising familiar from many commercial websites.

That restraint creates a different experience for the visitor. A page dominated by text and links places fewer visual demands on someone trying to complete a practical task. A person arrives because an apartment, a couch, or a job may be listed nearby. The useful result matters more than how long the person stays on the page.

## Why prettier rivals kept struggling

The history of classified advertising is also a history of disruption. Newspapers once depended heavily on classifieds, and the migration of those listings to a free or cheap web alternative is frequently cited in accounts of the newspaper industry's decline. Later, more specialized platforms carved off pieces of the Craigslist world, housing, jobs, dating, and goods each attracting venture backed challengers with modern design and focused tools.

Specialized marketplaces have created useful alternatives for particular transactions, but their arrival did not end Craigslist. A broad local board can still place housing, secondhand goods, community announcements, and work in one familiar setting. That range is part of its appeal, even for people who also use other services.

Plainness does not remove the problems associated with online classifieds. Scams, misleading listings, and personal safety concerns remain reasons to examine a transaction carefully. A familiar appearance can make a service comfortable to use without verifying every person or listing on it. Trust in the interface and trust in an individual transaction are separate things.

## What staying the same says about the web now

Craigslist occupies an unusual cultural position. It remains a working utility while preserving the appearance of an earlier web, one associated with links, text, and local lists. Its continued existence gives design writers a useful case study in the difference between a fashionable interface and an interface people already know how to use.

The shark comparison endures because it captures the importance of fit. A classified board does not necessarily need the features of a social feed. That does not mean every business can copy Craigslist’s appearance and expect its established audience to come with it. Its history, recognition, and accumulated local listings cannot be reproduced by choosing a plain font and blue links.

For a newer business, the relevant lesson is about making the purpose of a page clear. Design can help a visitor understand what a company does, find the information they need, and recognize where to make contact. Craigslist shows how consistency and usefulness can carry a service through changing fashions. It does not settle what another company’s website should look like.

# How QR Codes Became Part of Everyday Business

**Card photo:** `the-little-square-qr-code-comeback.png`

QR codes became a familiar part of American restaurant visits as smartphone cameras made scanning easier and the pandemic accelerated demand for contactless menus. The technology itself was much older and had already found widespread use in Japan. Its later American resurgence shows how a useful invention can gain a new audience when devices, habits, and circumstances align.

For many American consumers, the square once sat unnoticed in the corner of posters, receipts, and product boxes. It looked technical, like something meant for warehouses rather than everyday customers. Later, restaurant tables made it a routine part of deciding what to order. That change was dramatic in the United States, but it was not the beginning of the technology’s usefulness elsewhere.

The QR code’s American comeback is easier to understand when its history is separated from its reputation. It had a proven role in manufacturing long before diners encountered it on a menu stand. The experience around it changed as scanning became simpler, and a familiar criticism returned with its popularity, whether a digital shortcut actually makes life easier for the person using it.

## Born in a factory in Japan

DENSO WAVE introduced the QR code in Japan in 1994. It was developed for fast, reliable information reading and adopted in automotive production and related work. The letters stand for Quick Response. Its original purpose was practical, allowing information to move efficiently through settings where speed and accuracy mattered.

A QR code carries information across a two dimensional pattern rather than a single row of bars. The format can represent links, text, and other data in a compact space. Its recognizable corner patterns help a scanner identify orientation. Those capabilities explain why a design developed for industrial work could later become useful on packaging, tickets, and printed materials.

DENSO WAVE’s history records widespread public use in Japan in 2002, helped by mobile phones that could read the codes. That earlier adoption matters. The later restaurant boom was an expansion into a different setting and audience, not the rescue of a technology that everyone had rejected.

## The app problem and the long shrug

Early American attempts to bring QR codes to shoppers ran into a simple friction. A person who saw a code in a magazine or on a sign usually needed a separate app to scan it. Downloading software, opening it, and aiming it correctly, all to reach a website that might not be worth the effort, asked too much for too little reward. Many campaigns offered a dull destination at the end of that work, which taught people that scanning was rarely worth it.

The square acquired a reputation as a gimmick in some American marketing. A code placed where someone could not easily stop, scan, or get online created effort without much reward. The technology could work perfectly while the overall experience felt inconvenient. That gap helps explain why a successful industrial tool did not become an equally successful consumer habit everywhere at once.

Apple’s iOS 11 added system support for recognizing QR codes in Camera and Safari. Apple’s own developer material documents the feature. Removing the separate scanning app reduced the effort between noticing a code and opening its destination. It made the same printed square easier to use without changing what the square represented.

## The pandemic made it familiar at restaurant tables

During the pandemic, restaurants sought ways to reduce shared handling and adjust how they served guests. A printed code could link to a menu without requiring a reusable menu to pass from one person to another. Restaurants could also change a linked menu without replacing every printed copy. The code became a practical part of a wider adjustment to service.

Diners learned the process through repetition, hold up a phone, open the detected link, and read the menu. A March 2022 QSR Web commentary described rapid restaurant adoption and the operational appeal of digital menus. That account captures how operators were discussing the technology during the period. A tool that had once required explanation was becoming familiar through ordinary restaurant visits.

Familiarity helped the habit persist beyond the circumstances that accelerated it. Once someone has scanned menus repeatedly, the printed square no longer needs much explanation. That does not make it the preferred option for every diner. Convenience depends on the device, the connection, the person, and the menu that opens.

## The argument that never quite ended

Not everyone experienced the comeback as progress. The debate that started during the pandemic has continued in dining rooms, opinion columns, and everyday conversation, and it reveals larger tensions about phones at the table and who gets left out when a service moves onto a screen.

Some diners simply miss paper menus. A large printed menu can be shared, scanned at a glance, and read without pinching, zooming, or waiting for a page to load on a weak connection. There is also a social complaint. A meal out once began with everyone looking at the same kind of object. It now often begins with everyone looking down at separate phones, which can feel less like hospitality and more like homework before dinner.

Accessibility concerns remain a serious part of the discussion. A QR only menu assumes a guest has a charged smartphone, a data connection or venue WiFi, adequate vision for a small screen, and the comfort to navigate a mobile site. Older diners, people with visual impairments, and people without reliable devices can find the process frustrating or exclusionary. Many restaurants kept paper menus available on request, but critics note that having to ask for the accessible option is itself a different experience from being handed it as a matter of course.

Security adds another complication. The Federal Trade Commission has warned that harmful links can be concealed in QR codes and that scammers may cover a legitimate code with a different one. The square is a way of encoding information, not a visible guarantee that the destination is genuine. That distinction complicates the idea of scanning as a completely frictionless interaction.

## A square that waited for its moment

The QR code story resists a simple invention and triumph narrative. It began in industrial use, reached the Japanese public years before the pandemic, and later became more familiar to American diners. Those different stages show how adoption depends on context. A capability can already be useful somewhere while still feeling unnecessary somewhere else.

Its history also separates technical capability from a good customer experience. A code can be readable while the linked page is slow, confusing, or difficult to use on a phone. The printed square removes some effort only if the destination is useful. The customer still has to complete the task after the scan.

The strongest version of the technology is easy to overlook because it feels ordinary. A diner reaches a readable menu, gets the information needed, and returns attention to the meal. The weakest version turns a simple request into several technical obstacles. The debate continues because both experiences can begin with a square that looks almost identical.

# How Email Newsletters Became a Media Business Again

**Card photo:** `inbox-came-back-email-newsletters.png`

Email newsletters regained attention as independent writers and publishers looked for a more direct relationship with readers beyond social feeds. Subscription publishing made the inbox a place for reporting, commentary, and specialized knowledge as well as ordinary messages. That relationship can be more portable than a social following, although delivery, discovery, and earning a living still depend on more than having a list.

For a while, email seemed like the unglamorous utility closet of online life, work, spam, bills, and password resets. Social feeds appeared to be where public conversation and discovery happened. Around the newsletter boom of 2020, some prominent writers drew attention back to a quieter format, a publication delivered to people who had explicitly chosen to receive it.

The return of the email newsletter is one of the stranger revival stories in media. The format itself is not new and not glamorous. It has no feed to refresh and no video to autoplay. Its comeback says less about nostalgia than about exhaustion, economics, and a long argument over who really owns the relationship between a writer and the people who read the writer.

## An old format that faded in the feed years

Newsletters have existed for as long as email has been common, and in print form for far longer than that. Community groups, companies, columnists, and obsessive hobbyists all learned early that a regular message sent directly to interested people could hold a small public together. In the early 2010s, though, that format faded from the center of online culture as social feeds rose.

Facebook and Twitter, as the latter was then known, offered scale that email could not match in the moment. A post could travel far beyond its author's own list, carried by shares and algorithmic recommendation to people who had never heard the author's name. For publishers chasing growth, that promise was hard to resist. Writers built followings inside platforms, newsrooms organized their days around what traveled there, and the inbox came to feel private and workmanlike, a place for receipts rather than discovery.

A large social following did not give a writer control over how a platform distributed each post. A change in the service could alter the path between publication and discovery. The distinction was between having followers inside another company’s interface and having permission to contact subscribers through a channel that could be moved to another provider.

## Substack and the pitch of owning the list

Substack combined publishing, email distribution, and reader subscriptions in one service. Its appeal included the ability to keep a subscriber list rather than treating a platform following as the only connection to readers. Substack’s own documentation confirms that publishers can export their email lists, although portability still has practical and consent related limits.

That detail became central to the appeal. A subscriber list represents people who have chosen to hear from a publication. It can support a continuing relationship across changes in publishing software. Readers still choose whether to remain subscribed and whether to open what arrives. The list makes continuing contact possible, while the publication has to keep earning their attention.

NPR’s 2020 reporting examined writers building publications on Substack and the debate surrounding their independence. Prominent journalists helped attract attention to the model, but their existing reputations also complicated the success story. A writer arriving with a recognized name and an established audience begins in a different position from someone publishing their first issue. The discussion concerned editorial support, sustainable income, and how much an independent writer could realistically take on alone.

## The platforms enter the newsletter business

The established social platforms also pursued newsletters. Twitter announced its acquisition of Revue in January 2021, rather than inventing the service from scratch. The acquisition brought an existing editorial newsletter product into a platform already used by writers to find readers. It showed how closely social discovery and subscription publishing had become connected.

The later history belongs in the same story. Revue closed on January 18, 2023, according to the platform’s help center. Its acquisition and closure show that enthusiasm for newsletters did not guarantee the survival of every newsletter product. A durable format and the fortunes of a particular provider are separate subjects.

Substack introduced Notes in April 2023, adding a place for shorter posts and interaction. The boundaries between a newsletter service and a social platform became less clear. A reader could discover a writer through a feed and later choose to subscribe by email. The format did not eliminate the value of discovery elsewhere.

Critics of the newsletter boom raised pointed questions from the start. A paid inbox can become a bubble, rewarding writers for pleasing a loyal niche rather than challenging it. Without editors, errors and excesses travel with fewer checks. The economics that look liberating for a star with a hundred thousand free subscribers look very different for a talented newcomer whose list fits in a spreadsheet. Reporting on the era returned often to that divide, between a visible top tier whose success proved the model was possible and a much larger group for whom it remained precarious.

## Saturation and the crowded morning

A busy inbox also places limits on the idea of a direct relationship. Someone may want to read several publications but have time for only a few. An email delivered successfully can still sit unopened. For the reader, the competition is not just between newsletters, it includes work messages, family messages, receipts, and everything else that arrives in the same space.

A newsletter can feel personal without being the only publication a person values. Established newsrooms, independent writers, and specialist publishers all use the format for different purposes. Their audiences and resources vary, so one prominent writer’s success does not establish what a newcomer should expect. The recurring work of publishing remains, even when the distribution software is convenient.

## Why the inbox endures

Email has outlasted many predictions of its decline because it works across many providers. No single company owns the entire system. That openness supports portability, but individual mailbox services still filter and organize messages. Spam filtering, sender reputation, and reader behavior can affect delivery and attention. Substack’s own documentation warns that spam complaints can damage deliverability.

The newsletter revival did not require email to replace social media, websites, or other forms of publishing. It renewed interest in a channel where readers could choose a recurring publication and writers could maintain a contact list outside a single social profile. That is a meaningful difference in the relationship, without making it free of platforms, filtering, or competition.

The inbox remains a place where a publication can become part of someone’s routine. Its appeal rests on being invited in and continuing to earn that invitation. The technology is familiar, but the relationship still depends on the writing being worth a reader’s time. That is why the newsletter story is about publishing as much as it is about email.
