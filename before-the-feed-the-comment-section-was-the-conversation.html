Before the Feed, the Comment Section Was the Conversation

October 21, 2026

Scroll to the bottom of an old blog post that still allows replies and you can feel a familiar atmosphere in an unfamiliar place. Strangers are performing for each other. Regulars greet each other by username. Someone has arrived with a correction, someone else with a grudge, and a third person has written a reply longer and better sourced than the article above it. Likes, follows, pile ons, running jokes, reputations built one post at a time, all of it is there, humming along years before most of those readers had ever heard the phrase social media. Comment sections were among the places where the public web practiced large scale social conversation. Letters to the editor had always existed, but they moved at the speed of print, passed through an editors hands, and appeared days later, trimmed and tamed. A comment appeared in minutes, under your chosen name, in front of everyone. For readers who had grown up consuming media in silence, that small text box at the bottom of a page was a genuinely new civic experience, a chance to answer back in public and be answered in return.

From Guest Books to the Bottom of Every Page

The habits formed early. Personal sites in the 1990s kept guest books where visitors signed their names and left a line of praise or mischief. Forums and message boards gathered people around shared obsessions, from car repair to obscure bands, and taught the grammar that comment sections inherited, threaded replies, quoted passages, moderators with quiet power, and regulars whose posting history functioned as a biography. When blogs took off in the early 2000s, they carried that culture into publishing itself. A post was understood to be the start of a conversation, not the end of one, and a blogger with a lively comment thread had something no print columnist could buy, a room that stayed full after the author left it. News organizations arrived more cautiously and then all at once. Commenting promised engagement, return visits, and pages that kept growing after publication, and in an era when publishers were anxious about losing readers to the open web, a busy thread looked like loyalty made visible. Readers corrected errors, added local knowledge no reporter could have gathered alone, and sometimes argued a story into a better shape in public. Journalists who dove into their own threads, as Washington Post writer Chris Cillizza did on his political blog The Fix beginning in 2006, found devoted communities waiting there, at least at first. The same openness carried the seeds of the sections reputation. Anonymity, or the lighter shield of a pseudonym, lowered the cost of cruelty. Threads on hard stories attracted insults, conspiracy theories, and organized provocation. Research began to suggest that the damage was not confined to hurt feelings. A widely cited study led by Dominique Brossard and Dietram Scheufele at the University of Wisconsin Madison found that uncivil comments under a balanced article about nanotechnology polarized readers and shifted how they judged the risks described in the article itself. The conversation below the line, as British journalists came to call it, could quietly rewrite the story above it.

The Years When Publishers Walked Away

Popular Science made the break impossible to ignore. On September 24, 2013, the magazine announced that it would no longer accept comments on new articles, declaring plainly that comments can be bad for science. Online editor Suzanne LaBarre pointed to trolls and spambots, and to research showing that a fractious minority could skew a readers perception of solid reporting. A publication devoted to evidence had concluded that the evidence about its own comment section pointed in one direction, and it closed the door. Popular Science was early, but it was not alone for long. In a concentrated season of retreat, Reuters, Recode, Mic, The Week, and the Chicago Sun Times all shut or sharply reduced their comment sections in and around 2014, and many editors gave the same pair of reasons. Moderation consumed staff time and money that shrinking newsrooms believed belonged in reporting, and the conversation, they argued, had already moved. Readers were discussing stories on Facebook and Twitter, platforms that promised larger audiences and, at the time, an appealing story about someone else handling the ugliest parts of the job. NPR joined the departures on August 17, 2016, ending comments on its site after eight years and explaining that a tiny fraction of its audience produced the overwhelming bulk of its comments, with discussion thriving instead across its many social accounts. Gannett later closed comments across most of its local publications, describing sections that quickly devolve when left unmonitored. By one scholarly count cited in research on United States newspapers, at least 68 outlets closed comment sections between 2008 and the early 2020s. A feature that had once been sold as the democratic future of media had become, in the language of managers, a reputational hazard and a cost center.

What Was Lost in the Move to the Feed

Handing the conversation to social platforms solved a staffing problem and created a subtler one. On a publishers own site, a comment lives beside the work it answers, and the community that forms there belongs, in some meaningful sense, to that publication and its readers. In a feed, the discussion scatters. It is sorted by systems built to reward heat, detached from the article within hours, and addressed to followers rather than to fellow readers who arrived by the same road. Writers kept the insults, since feeds supplied plenty, and lost the regulars, the corrections desk that never slept, and the quiet reader who only ever spoke in that one room. Former defenders of the sections have been candid about the trade. Comment threads, at their best, taught writers things, surfaced errors that editors missed, and gave a publication a public that talked back in its own accent rather than in the flattened tone of a platform. At their worst, they mirrored everything people dislike about feeds, the baiting, the monologues, the handful of voices dominating every subject. The honest history holds both. The comment section was not a golden age ruined by a few bad actors. It was the original laboratory where the internet learned, in public, how hard it is for strangers to talk well at scale, and every social platform since has rerun those experiments with bigger budgets and similar results. The closures also revealed something about how media companies understood their readers. A comment section treats the audience as participants in a shared place. A feed treats the audience as distribution. Walking away from the first in favor of the second made a certain business sense in the middle of the 2010s, when social traffic looked endless, but it rested on rented ground, and publishers learned in later years how quickly a platform can change its terms, its algorithm, or its appetite for news.

The Quiet Return, Behind a Paywall

The most interesting recent chapter is not a restoration but a reinvention. In late 2024, The Washington Post relaunched commenting for subscribers with promises of meaningful, high quality discourse. Wired presented a new commenting experience as part of its subscription offering, a place where readers could connect with writers. The Financial Times has invested in automated moderation that nudges discussions toward substance and draws in readers who rarely comment. In this new model, the comment section is no longer a free town square bolted onto the bottom of an article. It is a members room, part of what a subscription buys, moderated with better tools and, crucially, with a business reason to keep it civil and alive. That shift would have puzzled the bloggers of 2004, for whom openness was the entire point. Yet it also concedes what the closure era tried to deny, that readers value talking where they read, and that a well kept conversation is worth paying to maintain. The technology of moderation has changed too. Automated systems that once strained to catch crude profanity now sort tone, flag bad faith patterns, and surface strong comments that human moderators might never have reached in time. Whether the members room version can recover the democratic messiness that made the original sections matter is an open argument, and it is a fitting one. The comment section began as the webs first experiment in giving readers a microphone, grew into a burden publishers were relieved to surrender, and returned as a product feature once its absence started costing loyalty. Through every phase, the underlying desire never moved. People finish reading something that moved them, angered them, or taught them something, and they want to say so to other people who just read the same words. Platforms rise and collapse around that desire. The small text box at the bottom of the page was simply the first place it found a home.
