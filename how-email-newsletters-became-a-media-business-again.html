How Email Newsletters Became a Media Business Again

October 15, 2026

Email newsletters regained attention as independent writers and publishers looked for a more direct relationship with readers beyond social feeds. Subscription publishing made the inbox a place for reporting, commentary, and specialized knowledge as well as ordinary messages. That relationship can be more portable than a social following, although delivery, discovery, and earning a living still depend on more than having a list.

For a while, email seemed like the unglamorous utility closet of online life, work, spam, bills, and password resets. Social feeds appeared to be where public conversation and discovery happened. Around the newsletter boom of 2020, some prominent writers drew attention back to a quieter format, a publication delivered to people who had explicitly chosen to receive it.

The return of the email newsletter is one of the stranger revival stories in media. The format itself is not new and not glamorous. It has no feed to refresh and no video to autoplay. Its comeback says less about nostalgia than about exhaustion, economics, and a long argument over who really owns the relationship between a writer and the people who read the writer.

An old format that faded in the feed years

Newsletters have existed for as long as email has been common, and in print form for far longer than that. Community groups, companies, columnists, and obsessive hobbyists all learned early that a regular message sent directly to interested people could hold a small public together. In the early 2010s, though, that format faded from the center of online culture as social feeds rose.

Facebook and Twitter, as the latter was then known, offered scale that email could not match in the moment. A post could travel far beyond its author's own list, carried by shares and algorithmic recommendation to people who had never heard the author's name. For publishers chasing growth, that promise was hard to resist. Writers built followings inside platforms, newsrooms organized their days around what traveled there, and the inbox came to feel private and workmanlike, a place for receipts rather than discovery.

A large social following did not give a writer control over how a platform distributed each post. A change in the service could alter the path between publication and discovery. The distinction was between having followers inside another company’s interface and having permission to contact subscribers through a channel that could be moved to another provider.

Substack and the pitch of owning the list

Substack combined publishing, email distribution, and reader subscriptions in one service. Its appeal included the ability to keep a subscriber list rather than treating a platform following as the only connection to readers. Substack’s own documentation confirms that publishers can export their email lists, although portability still has practical and consent related limits.

That detail became central to the appeal. A subscriber list represents people who have chosen to hear from a publication. It can support a continuing relationship across changes in publishing software. Readers still choose whether to remain subscribed and whether to open what arrives. The list makes continuing contact possible, while the publication has to keep earning their attention.

NPR’s 2020 reporting examined writers building publications on Substack and the debate surrounding their independence. Prominent journalists helped attract attention to the model, but their existing reputations also complicated the success story. A writer arriving with a recognized name and an established audience begins in a different position from someone publishing their first issue. The discussion concerned editorial support, sustainable income, and how much an independent writer could realistically take on alone.

The platforms enter the newsletter business

The established social platforms also pursued newsletters. Twitter announced its acquisition of Revue in January 2021, rather than inventing the service from scratch. The acquisition brought an existing editorial newsletter product into a platform already used by writers to find readers. It showed how closely social discovery and subscription publishing had become connected.

The later history belongs in the same story. Revue closed on January 18, 2023, according to the platform’s help center. Its acquisition and closure show that enthusiasm for newsletters did not guarantee the survival of every newsletter product. A durable format and the fortunes of a particular provider are separate subjects.

Substack introduced Notes in April 2023, adding a place for shorter posts and interaction. The boundaries between a newsletter service and a social platform became less clear. A reader could discover a writer through a feed and later choose to subscribe by email. The format did not eliminate the value of discovery elsewhere.

Critics of the newsletter boom raised pointed questions from the start. A paid inbox can become a bubble, rewarding writers for pleasing a loyal niche rather than challenging it. Without editors, errors and excesses travel with fewer checks. The economics that look liberating for a star with a hundred thousand free subscribers look very different for a talented newcomer whose list fits in a spreadsheet. Reporting on the era returned often to that divide, between a visible top tier whose success proved the model was possible and a much larger group for whom it remained precarious.

Saturation and the crowded morning

A busy inbox also places limits on the idea of a direct relationship. Someone may want to read several publications but have time for only a few. An email delivered successfully can still sit unopened. For the reader, the competition is not just between newsletters, it includes work messages, family messages, receipts, and everything else that arrives in the same space.

A newsletter can feel personal without being the only publication a person values. Established newsrooms, independent writers, and specialist publishers all use the format for different purposes. Their audiences and resources vary, so one prominent writer’s success does not establish what a newcomer should expect. The recurring work of publishing remains, even when the distribution software is convenient.

Why the inbox endures

Email has outlasted many predictions of its decline because it works across many providers. No single company owns the entire system. That openness supports portability, but individual mailbox services still filter and organize messages. Spam filtering, sender reputation, and reader behavior can affect delivery and attention. Substack’s own documentation warns that spam complaints can damage deliverability.

The newsletter revival did not require email to replace social media, websites, or other forms of publishing. It renewed interest in a channel where readers could choose a recurring publication and writers could maintain a contact list outside a single social profile. That is a meaningful difference in the relationship, without making it free of platforms, filtering, or competition.

The inbox remains a place where a publication can become part of someone’s routine. Its appeal rests on being invited in and continuing to earn that invitation. The technology is familiar, but the relationship still depends on the writing being worth a reader’s time. That is why the newsletter story is about publishing as much as it is about email.
